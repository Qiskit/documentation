# Modern Workflows

This course has already covered a wide variety of methods for handling quantum noise and errors. We have intentionally avoided some black-box solutions so that we can see how these methods work. But now we are ready to embrace these methods in their most modern form. Specifically, we explore some highly optimized error-handling tools that hide some complexity for us, and on the other hand, we explore Samplomatic and addons like shaded light cones (SLC) which give us very fine control over certain mitigation processes.

## Overview

Early Qiskit Runtime primitives such as Estimator and Sampler exposed error mitigation through a small number of preset levels. Later releases provided more direct control over individual techniques, including choices of zero-noise extrapolation strategy, Pauli twirling configurations, and randomization parameters. However, these settings remained largely global, applying uniformly across an entire circuit.

![A diagram showing that different phases of execution models have increased user control.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/phases.avif)

As quantum workloads become more sophisticated, developers increasingly need the ability to tailor execution and mitigation strategies to specific regions of a circuit. The diagram below outlines several key tools discussed in this lesson and shows where they fit within a typical workflow.

![A diagram that workflows typically progress from mapping problems to quantum circuits, transpilation and optimization, execution and post-processing, and that each of these steps has modern tools associated with them.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/workflow.avif)

The directed execution model addresses the need for modular control over execution and the integration of many of these tools.

### Directed execution model

Rather than making implicit execution decisions, the directed execution model follows user-provided directives. This improves transparency and makes complex workflows easier to inspect and debug. Some key features of this model include:

* Per-layer configuration through labeled circuit regions
* Identification of unique and repeated layers for efficient noise learning
* Batched execution workflows
* Structured post-processing
* Close integration with add-ons such as shaded lightcones

The model is built around two key components:

* Samplomatic, which defines execution recipes
* Executor, which carries out those recipes and returns samples

Most workflows that use Executor could also be implemented with Sampler, since both ultimately produce samples. The key advantage is efficiency. Tasks such as generating twirling configurations are often much faster when performed in the server-side near-time environment rather than on the client. Estimator and Sampler remain supported and might still be the most convenient choice for many workloads.

Before execution, however, a circuit must first be constructed and prepared. Qiskit provides several add-ons that assist with circuit generation, simulation, and optimization. Follow the links for more information on any of the tools below.

### Building Quantum Circuits

For some applications or brand new approaches, users must figure out how to map their problem to a quantum circuit themselves. In many domains, however, Qiskit add-ons can automate important parts of this process.

#### Optimization Mapper

[Optimization Mapper](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-addon-opt-mapper/) helps express classical optimization problems in forms suitable for quantum algorithms. Key functionality includes:

* Construct objective functions and constraints
* Convert problems into QUBO formulations
* Generate Hamiltonian or operator representations

![An image of a molecule and graph being mapped to qubits.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/mappers.avif)

#### Fermionic Mapper

[Fermionic Mapper](/docs/addons/qiskit-fermions) converts fermionic operators into qubit operators suitable for quantum hardware. It supports mappings such as Jordan-Wigner, which converts operators on indistinguishable fermions to Pauli operators on distinguishable qubits, while preserving fermionic statistics. This addon is useful for quantum chemistry and materials science applications.

The previous two addons were focused on the initial mapping of a problem structure to a quantum circuit. But it is equally important to efficiently map the time evolution of a process onto a quantum circuit, and to optimize a circuit by reducing its depth. The next few circuit addons address this.

#### Multiproduct Formulas (MPF)

[Multiproduct formulas](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-addon-mpf/) improve the accuracy of quantum time-evolution simulations by combining multiple approximations. More specifically, one creates circuits with a varying number of Trotter steps so that a weighted combination of circuit executions can be used. For a given target accuracy, MPF can often use shallower circuits than standard Trotter methods. This comes at the cost of executing multiple circuits and combining their results classically.

![An image showing trotter circuits with different numbers of steps.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/mpf.avif)

#### AQC Tensor

[AQC Tensor](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-addon-aqc-tensor/) performs approximate quantum compilation using tensor-network methods. A common use case is replacing a portion of a deep circuit with a shallower variational approximation. The resulting circuit can achieve similar accuracy while significantly reducing depth.

![An image showing the first few layers of a quantum circuit being compressed using AQC.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/aqc.avif)

### Transpilation

You have already used transpilation many times in this course. You know that once an abstract circuit has been constructed, it must be prepared for execution on hardware. But we have not delved deep into the multiple stages of transpilation.

Key transpilation stages include:

* Layout: map logical qubits to physical qubits
* Translation: rewrite operations using the backend's native gate set
* Routing: insert and optimize SWAP operations required by hardware connectivity

These processes are interrelated. For example, the layout should be chosen in a way that minimizes depth even once routing is taken into account. Also, layout should be chosen so as to avoid the most error-prone qubits or connections. These stages are often automated, but advanced users might choose to specify custom layouts or other constraints when needed.

![An image showing how the qubits in an abstract quantum circuit need to be assigned to real physical qubits on a quantum computer as part of transpilation.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/transp-mapping.avif)

To learn more about transpilation, see the [guide on IBM Quantum](/docs/guides/transpile).

### Reducing the effects of noise and errors

Several Qiskit add-ons focus specifically on reducing the impact of noise and hardware imperfections.

#### Operator Backpropagation and Noise-Aware Circuit Transformations

We have already discussed operator propagation in the context of coherent Pauli checks in error correction. But operator propagation is more generally useful. There are two main ways of leveraging the propagation of an operation through a quantum circuit: (1) absorb the last few layers of a quantum circuit into the observables being measured, and (2) take into account the likely effects of errors in the circuit and compensate for them by measuring a different observable. These approaches are leveraged in the next two tools.

#### Operator Backpropagation

[Operator Backpropagation](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-addon-cutting/) reduces circuit depth by absorbing gates from the end of a circuit into the measured observable. For a simple example, consider the circuit below.

![An image showing a quantum circuit with several entangling and rotation layers, followed by a ladder of CNOTs from qubit 0 down to qubit 5. Finally, a Z measurement is made on qubit 5.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/backprop.avif)

Here, there are rotation and entangling layers that appear non-trivial, followed by a ladder of entanglement and a measurement of `Z` on qubit 5. This circuit has a two-qubit depth of 7. This is prior to transpilation, and this process would normally be done after transpilation, but this is merely a pedagogical example. Recall that there are rules for propagating an observable past a gate, and in particular, when a CNOT gate has target $q_t$ and control $q_c$, a $Z_t$ observable on $q_t$ becomes $Z_c Z_t$ when propagated backward past the CNOT: $Z_t \rightarrow Z_c Z_t$. In the case of this example circuit, propagating the observable $Z_5$ back through the entire ladder of CNOTs yields the observable $Z_5 Z_4 Z_3 Z_2 Z_1 Z_0$ or in the standard Qiskit notation, simply $ZZZZZZ$. Measuring this observable instead of just $Z_5$ allows us to remove the entire ladder of CNOTs, reducing the two-qubit depth from 7 to just 2! This still only requires a measurement in one basis on each qubit, so it would not even increase the number of shots that we require. This is not always the case. Generally, more complicated observables require more shots, and sometimes more measurements in different bases.

The overall strategy of operator backpropagation is to:

* Shorten the executed circuit
* Replace the original observable with an expanded observable
* Trade circuit depth for increased sampling requirements

The circuit above is an obvious example where using backpropagation is advantageous, but circuits that would benefit optimally from backpropagation are not generally trivial to identify. Determining when to use backpropagation will likely require checking to see how much depth can be cut from your circuit and balancing this with the increased sampling requirements.

#### Propagated Noise Absorption (PNA)

[Propagated Noise Absorption](/docs/addons/qiskit-addon-pna) is structurally similar to operator backpropagation, but rather than propagating an operator back through the ideal gates in a circuit, one incorporates a learned noise model and propagates the effects of noise directly into the observable. PNA:

* Learns noise behavior from the hardware
* Propagates the noise model through the circuit
* Produces a modified observable that compensates for gate noise

![An image showing the gate errors on many layers of a quantum circuit being propagated forward to the final observable measurement.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/pna.avif)

When the modified observable is measured on noisy hardware, the resulting expectation values better approximate the ideal noiseless result. Here, there are two types of overhead: firstly, you need to learn the noise model and this requires running additional circuits, and secondly this can produce more complicated observables, which can increase sampling requirements.

#### Circuit Cutting

[Circuit Cutting](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-addon-cutting/) partitions a large circuit into smaller subcircuits that can be executed independently. Sufficient measurements are then made to stitch the results back together. Circuit cutting:

* Reduces hardware requirements
* Allows computations larger than available qubit counts (wider circuits)
* Helps address depth and connectivity limitations (deeper circuits)
* Reconstructs the original result through classical post-processing

![An image showing two circuits being cut, so that they can be run separately and recombined later in post-processing.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/cutting.avif)

### Samplomatic

Many of the techniques in the previous sections complement other suppression and mitigation methods covered throughout this course, including:

* Dynamical decoupling
* Pauli twirling
* M3
* TREX
* Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)
* Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)

In many workflows, different techniques can be applied to different regions of the same circuit. And as we have seen, many of the techniques come with quantum overhead. We therefore sometimes need fine, detailed control over noise learning, sampling, and application of twirling sequences. These are some of the motivations behind Samplomatic.

[Samplomatic](/docs/addons/samplomatic) is an open-source library that enables customized sampling, randomization, and error-mitigation workflows.

Its first task is to analyze a circuit and divide it into labeled regions, or boxes. These boxes serve two purposes:
* They visually partition the circuit.
* They create indexed regions that can be configured independently.

Users can attach annotations that describe how each box should be treated during execution. Examples include:
* Applying Pauli twirling to all boxes
* Applying twirling only to selected boxes
* Using different twirling groups in different regions
* Injecting learned noise into specific layers
* Customizing mitigation strategies at a fine-grained level

Samplomatic also identifies repeated boxed regions. Because identical layers typically exhibit similar noise characteristics, noise learning only needs to be performed once per unique layer.

#### Samplomatic Outputs

Samplomatic produces two primary objects:

* Template circuit
* Samplex

The template circuit is a boxed, transpiled, parameterized circuit ready for execution. The samplex contains all information required to generate and process circuit variants. Samplex contents include:

* Parameter values for parameterized circuits
* Randomization selections
* Sampling distributions
* Post-processing information
* Additional execution metadata

Formally, a samplex is a probability distribution over the parameters of a template circuit together with auxiliary information required for execution and post-processing. An important practical advantage is that both the template circuit and samplex are created locally on the client side. This allows inspection, validation, and debugging before any hardware resources are consumed.

There are many benefits of Samplomatic including fine-grained control of things like adaptive shot allocation and randomizations. There are also logistical benefits like the structured post-processing workflows. One of the most striking benefits, though, is the reuse of learned noise information across identical layers, which can save a lot of quantum computing time in the noise learning process.

### Shaded Lightcones

Before deciding where to apply expensive mitigation techniques, however, it is helpful to identify the regions of a circuit that matter most to the observable being measured. This motivates the Shaded Lightcone add-on. The idea of a lightcone is borrowed from relativity, where causal relationships are constrained by the finite speed at which information can travel.

![An image of spacetime. Time runs from bottom to top, space runs side-to-side. Because no causation can move through space faster than the speed of light, the region of spacetime that an event can causally influence is limited to a conical region. This is an analogy for the spreading of errors in a quantum circuit.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/lightcone.avif)

A similar concept applies in quantum circuits.

Consider a large circuit in which only a low-weight observable is of interest, meaning observables that act nontrivially on only a handful of qubits. There are many reasons for interest in low-weight observables, for example:

* Individual terms within a larger Hamiltonian
* A localized interaction region
* Information collected onto a small set of ancilla qubits
* Other observables involving only a few qubits

Not every error in the circuit can affect the measurement outcome. This is especially true of Pauli errors, or any error that can be modeled as a set of Pauli operations. Look at this circuit, for example:

![A quantum circuit with an error just before measurement on qubit zero, but a measurement is only performed on qubit 6. There is no entangling gate between qubits 0 and 6 in that final layer.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/lightcone-a.avif)

A Pauli error occurring on qubit zero in the final layer cannot influence the measurement on qubit 6; there is no connecting gate that could propagate the error from qubit 0 to qubit 6. Note that this argument does not apply to certain non-unitary errors. A T1-like transition to the ground state on qubit 0 would collapse qubit 0 to a computational basis state, and if qubits 0 and 6 had previously been entangled (as they were in this circuit) then the non-unitary error in qubit 0 could indeed affect qubit 6. The following discussion focuses on errors that can be represented as Pauli operations, or probabilistic mixtures of Pauli operations

In the last layer, only a Pauli error on qubits 5 or 6 could directly affect the measurement on qubit 6.

In the entangling layer before that one, qubits 5 and 6 are entangled with qubits 4 and 7, respectively. So in that entangling layer, or just before it, a Pauli error on any of those four qubits could affect the later measurement. One can continue tracing backward through the circuit to reveal the subset of gates and qubits that are causally connected to the measurement. This causal set forms a cone-shaped region in space (qubits) and time (layers), giving rise to the term lightcone. The exact shape of the region varies from circuit to circuit. Because this analysis depends on hardware connectivity and gate placement, it is most useful after transpilation, routing, and layout optimization.

#### Beyond Causal Connectivity

**Shaded** Lightcones extend this idea further by considering which types of errors matter.

Note for example:

* A Z error immediately before a Z-basis measurement has no effect on the measurement outcome.
* An X error immediately before a Z-basis measurement can dramatically change the result.

Some error types therefore deserve more mitigation effort than others. This distinction can substantially reduce the overhead of techniques such as PEC. In practice, separate sensitivity analyses are generated for:

* X errors
* Z errors
* Y (or XZ up to a phase) errors

This error sensitivity generally looks different for each error type, because different causally connected errors have different impacts on the measurement. Note also that the sensitivity changes throughout the circuit. An X error immediately before measurement might be highly damaging, while an error occurring many layers earlier might have a smaller effect after propagating through a highly entangled state.

![Three images showing the same quantum circuit, but with (a) no error, (b) an error just before measurement, and (c) an error earlier in the circuit.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/sensitivity.avif)

Shaded Lightcones quantify this difference by assigning a sensitivity value to potential errors throughout the circuit.

The resulting maps indicate:

* Where errors matter
* Which error types matter
* How strongly they influence the final observable

![Three images showing heat maps of error sensitivity to three different error types: X, Y, and Z.](/learning/images/courses/tools-for-quantum-advantage/modern-workflows/slc-xyz.avif)

Shaded lightcones do not mitigate errors themselves, but they can help focus mitigation effort where it provides the greatest benefit. 

### Learning and Injecting Noise

After a circuit has been constructed, optimized, and annotated, two complementary tasks remain: learning the noise and using the noise model (injecting noise or propagating the effects of noise, for example). 

#### NoiseLearnerV3

NoiseLearnerV3 is the current tool used for noise learning. The previous NoiseLearner interface accepted entire circuits and produced Pauli-Lindblad noise models. NoiseLearnerV3 instead operates directly on boxed circuit instructions. In short, NoiseLearnerV3:

* Accepts collections of labeled circuit layers
* Reuses information from repeated layers
* Supports measurement-noise learning
* Supports post-processing of learned models
* Enables noise injection and scaling workflows


This design aligns closely with Samplomatic's layer-oriented workflow. In particular, for each instruction set:
* Pauli-Lindblad learning is used for gate operations.
* TREX-based learning is used for measurements.

A learned noise model can then be used in a variety of methods we have already covered. For example, recall that using zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) with gate-folding can lead to intractably deep circuits. The alternative method for scaling noise in that case was to sample from a known noise model, effectively dialing up the noise level, even by non-integer factors. This probabilistic error amplification (PEA) is a prime example of how a noise model can be used to mitigate errors. Of course, a good noise model is also required for probabilistic error cancellation (PEC), and for propagated noise absorption (PNA).

In short, with a high-quality noise model, noise becomes a programmable resource rather than merely a source of error.

#### Shaded Lightcones vs. NoiseLearnerV3

NoiseLearnerV3 and SLC are closely related, but they take distinct inputs and answer different questions. SLC determines where mitigation effort should be focused. NoiseLearnerV3 characterizes the actual noise present in those regions. These different processes also involve different kinds of overhead. This is summarized in the table below.


|   | Shaded Lightcones	| NoiseLearnerV3 |
|---|-------------------|----------------|
| Purpose | Identifies where mitigation matters | Learns how errors behave|
| Overhead | Primarily classical overhead	| Requires hardware executions|
| Output | Produces sensitivity maps	| Produces noise-channel descriptions|
| Input | Uses circuit structure and operator propagation	| Uses circuit structure and experimental hardware data|

### Executor and Runtime Execution

Executor performs the final execution step. It can operate on either:

* A template circuit and samplex generated by Samplomatic
* A conventional quantum circuit without Samplomatic

This flexibility allows users to adopt as many or as few components of the directed execution workflow as needed.

Executor works closely with Samplomatic, which manages randomization choices, shot allocation, noise-injection selections, and related execution metadata as described above.

For practical examples of the directed execution model, see [documentation](/docs/guides), [tutorials](/docs/tutorials), and [addons guides](/docs/guides/addons) on the [IBM Quantum platform](https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/).

## Conclusion

Modern quantum workflows increasingly rely on a collection of complementary tools rather than a single execution primitive.

These tools support every stage of the workflow:

* Problem mapping
* Circuit construction
* Transpilation and optimization
* Error suppression
* Error mitigation
* Noise learning
* Structured execution
* Post-processing

Together, tools such as Samplomatic, Executor, NoiseLearnerV3, Shaded Lightcones, Propagated Noise Absorption, Circuit Cutting, and AQC Tensor provide significantly more control than earlier execution models.

The field continues to evolve rapidly. As quantum processors become larger and more connected, these techniques are likely to play an increasingly important role in achieving useful quantum advantage and, eventually, integrating seamlessly with fault-tolerant quantum computing workflows.
