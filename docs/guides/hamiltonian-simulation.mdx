# Hamiltonian simulation

Hamiltonian simulation is a quantum computing technique used to model and characterize quantum systems based on their energy and interactions. It has potential applications in drug discovery and chemistry, battery development, catalyst design, advanced materials, solar energy, and the study of complex physical phenomena such as superconductivity. 

By simulating molecular interactions and the behavior of electrons and atoms, Hamiltonian simulation can help researchers predict material properties, understand chemical reactions, and identify promising candidates for new technologies. Common approaches include Trotterization, which approximates continuous time evolution using sequences of quantum gates, and sample based quantum diagonalization, which estimates the energy spectrum for a given quantum system.

A typical workflow starts by mapping a physical system, such as a set of interacting fermions or bosons, onto qubits and circuits. From there, you can reduce the resulting circuit's depth and gate count with compilation and error-mitigation techniques, run the circuit on quantum hardware, and reconstruct the system's properties, such as its ground-state energy, from the measured samples. The tools on this page cover each of these stages, and the application function listed here provides a single managed workflow for quantum chemistry.

## Mapping tools
These capabilities specialize in mapping domain problems into quantum operators and circuits for execution on a quantum computer. Use them to represent fermionic or bosonic systems with dedicated data structures, and to compile the resulting circuits so that they run with less depth and fewer entangling gates on quantum hardware.

## Quantum subspace diagonalization tools
These tools provide capabilities for building diagonalization workflows and optimizing them for use in HPC environments. Rather than relying solely on the quantum circuit to generate a final answer, these methods use the circuit to sample candidate configurations, then classically diagonalize the system's Hamiltonian in the reduced subspace spanned by those configurations, so you can recover an energy estimate even when hardware noise would otherwise corrupt a direct measurement.

## Workflow validation tools
These capabilities are used for validating workflows through efficient simulation. Because certain classes of fermionic circuits can be simulated efficiently on classical hardware, you can use these tools to check a workflow, such as a particle-conserving ansatz, against a classical reference result before running it on quantum hardware.
